Debt-to-Income Ratio: Calculation, Uses and Limits

By MoneyMooring Editorial Team · Updated July 28, 2026 · 7 min readEducational content · Official sources checked July 28, 2026

Editorial disclosure: this guide is educational and is not individual financial, legal, tax or insurance advice. MoneyMooring does not sell or recommend a specific financial product.
Key takeaways
  • DTI divides monthly debt payments by gross monthly income.
  • Use required monthly debt payments, not account balances, in the basic calculation.
  • Lender limits vary by product and underwriting rules.
  • DTI excludes many living costs, so a manageable lender ratio can still feel unaffordable.

How to calculate monthly debt-to-income ratio, what lenders may use it for and why it is not a complete household budget. The examples below are explanatory, not product quotes or promises of approval, savings, coverage or investment performance.

The formula

DTI = total required monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100. Gross income is generally income before taxes and payroll deductions.

Using the CFPB’s example structure, if required monthly debt payments total $2,000 and gross monthly income is $6,000, DTI is 33.3%.

What usually enters the numerator

  • Mortgage or rent when required by the lender’s calculation
  • Auto, student and personal loan payments
  • Credit card minimum payments
  • Court-ordered obligations when applicable

Groceries, utilities, childcare, taxes and insurance may not appear in the standard total even though they strongly affect affordability.

Use two calculations

Calculate lender-style DTI, then build a separate cash-flow test using take-home income and all essential expenses. If the proposed payment works in one calculation but not the other, the household budget is the more important warning.

Stress-test the cash-flow version with a realistic increase in insurance, utilities or another variable cost. Also test a month with lower overtime, commission or self-employment income. These scenarios do not change the lender formula, but they show whether the payment leaves enough room for ordinary volatility.

Prepare a lender-ready record

List each debt with the creditor, current balance, required monthly payment and the statement date. For credit cards, use the required payment shown on a recent statement rather than dividing the balance by an assumed term. For installment loans, confirm whether a payment will end before the proposed loan closes because underwriting treatment can depend on the remaining term and lender rules.

Income documentation also matters. Salaried income may be straightforward, while bonuses, overtime, commissions, rental income or self-employment earnings can require a longer record and adjustments. Do not treat a personal calculation as an approval estimate: the lender will verify documents and apply its own product rules.

  • Use recent statements and pay records.
  • Calculate current DTI before adding a proposed payment.
  • Calculate the ratio again with the proposed payment included.
  • Compare both results with a full take-home cash-flow budget.
  • Keep an emergency reserve outside the down payment or loan proceeds.

Use the guide for a documented decision

Before acting on Debt-to-Income Ratio: Calculation, Uses and Limits, write down the facts that apply to your household: the current balance or coverage, the relevant deadline, the exact contract or account terms and the amount your budget can support. Then compare those facts with the official sources below and the latest documents from the institution, insurer, employer or government agency involved.

  • Save the dated statement, disclosure, policy or plan document used in the comparison.

  • Record the question that remains unresolved and who can answer it.
  • Recheck the numbers after a rate, balance, income, law or household change.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one maximum DTI for every loan?

No. The CFPB notes that different products and lenders use different limits and underwriting rules.

Does paying off a credit card help DTI?

It can reduce the required monthly debt payment used in the numerator, but the exact underwriting treatment depends on the lender.

Should rent be included?

Mortgage underwriting commonly evaluates housing and total debt ratios. For personal planning, include housing in the broader cash-flow test even if a specific lender formula differs.
